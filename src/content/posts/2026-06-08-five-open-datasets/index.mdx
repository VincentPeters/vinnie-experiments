Travel by Trees plans bike routes through Antwerp. The routes prefer parks, trees, and water over the fast grey road. To bias a route toward green, the app needs to know how green every street is.

No open dataset ships a greenness column. You build one.

This post covers the first half: where the data comes from. Five public sources, one PostGIS database, one enriched road network. The scoring comes next.

## Source 1: OpenStreetMap

The base map is OpenStreetMap. The Belgium extract from Geofabrik is about 650 MB, far more than one city needs.

A bounding box around Antwerp crops it down. One command with `osmium` turns 650 MB into 17 MB.

```bash title="Clip Belgium to Antwerp"
osmium extract --bbox 4.30,51.15,4.55,51.28 \
  --strategy complete_ways \
  belgium-latest.osm.pbf -o antwerp.pbf
```

`complete_ways` keeps roads whole even where they cross the box edge, so a street near the border still routes. From this one file come both the road network and, later, the parks and water.

## Source 2: the Groenkaart vegetation raster

OpenStreetMap knows where parks are. It does not know where the trees are.

For that there is the Groenkaart, a one-metre vegetation raster from Agentschap Natuur en Bos. Every pixel holds a class: tall green (trees above three metres), low green (shrubs and grass), farmland, or not green. It uses the Belgian Lambert projection, EPSG:31370, not the lon-lat of OpenStreetMap. That difference matters in the next post.

The web service caps each request at a few thousand pixels per side. Antwerp at one metre is far larger. So the fetch tiles the area into 4000-metre squares, downloads each, and stitches them back with `gdalbuildvrt`.

Loaded into PostGIS with `raster2pgsql`, cut into 256-pixel tiles with a spatial index, the raster becomes something you query per street.

### What the raster holds

The raster sorts Antwerp into four classes over 252 million classified pixels. Most of the city is not green at all. Toggle what you count as green and watch the coverage move.

<PyDemo
  title="Green coverage of Antwerp, by class"

    print_md(f"{name}: {pct:.1f}%")

print_md(f"counted as green: {green:.1f}% of the city")
`}
/>

A fifth of Antwerp is tall canopy. Over half is built surface. That scarcity is why the next post weights tall green fully and low green at a fifth, and why a green route is worth planning at all.

## Sources 3 and 4: parks, water, and 104,000 trees

Parks and water come from the same Antwerp extract. An `osm2pgsql` flex schema, written in Lua, sorts the green tags into one table: leisure parks, land-use green, natural green, and water.

The tree inventory is separate. Stad Antwerpen publishes every public tree, about 104,000 of them, through an ArcGIS service. The fetch walks the service two thousand records at a time and writes them out as GeoJSON points. Those points feed the "trees you passed" counter you see while riding.

## Source 5: air quality, on the bench

The last layer is NO2 from ATMO-Street, a ten-metre air-quality model from IRCEL-CELINE. Pollution between two streets a block apart varies by a factor of three.

It is on the map as a layer. It does not steer routes yet. Pretending it did would be the wrong kind of honest, so this one waits for a later post.

## Limits

This is Antwerp only. Wallonia has no Groenkaart, so the approach stops at the language border. The data is a snapshot, not a live feed. The tree inventory is points, not canopy, so a wide old plane tree counts the same as a sapling. And NO2 sits out of the routing for now.

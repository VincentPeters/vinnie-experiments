## The question

The score lives in a database column. A router does not read your database. It reads a routing graph: nodes, edges, and a cost on each edge. So the question is narrow. How does a per-street greenness class become a cheaper edge?

## Precompute, do not compute

The score is expensive to work out and never changes between requests. So it is worked out once, offline, and written into the map.

The class goes onto each road as a tag, `tbt_green`. BRouter then reads that tagged map and builds its own routing format: binary segment files. A three-step build cuts the map, unifies node positions, and links the ways into segments.

A 17 MB map of Antwerp becomes one 759 KB segment file. The router loads it at startup. Every route after that is a graph search over edges it already holds.

## Green edges cost less

BRouter routes by cost. The cheapest path wins. So to prefer green, you make green edges cheaper.

The profile gives each class a discount. A road inside a park has its cost cut by 80 percent. A high or medium green road gets 20 percent off. A car-free path gets a smaller bonus on top.

```text title="trees.brf, the discounts"
park    -> 0.80   off
high    -> 0.20   off
medium  -> 0.20   off
carfree -> 0.20   bonus, added at half weight
```

The discount multiplies the edge cost. A park path at full strength costs a fifth of its grey-road price, so the search bends toward it.

One detail worth its own line. The car-free bonus is added at half weight, under the green discount, on purpose. An earlier version let a cranked car-free bonus send routes onto an ungreen but car-free cycleway, in one case the ring road. Greenness has to dominate. Car-free only nudges.

## The slider, live

The slider in the map runs from 0 to 100. It maps to an intensity the profile reads, from 0 to 3. At 0 the discounts vanish and you get the plain fast route. At 100 they hit full strength.

BRouter re-reads its profile on every request. So the slider changes routing with no rebuild. Move it, and the same start and end re-plan against a different cost.

Two routes come back for each request. The green one, from the trees profile. The grey one, from a plain fast-bike profile. Both times are computed at one fixed speed, so "12 percent greener, 2 minutes longer" is a fair trade and not two routers arguing about their own clocks. The green share itself comes from the tags: the fraction of the route that runs on park, high, or medium streets.

## Routing through the park

One stubborn case. Middelheim's nicest paths are tagged `bicycle=no`. The router, correctly, refuses them. A green-routing app wants them anyway.

So there is a switch, off by default. With it on, the router considers park-interior paths at walking pace when the street is a park. It is the gap between what the map says you may do and what you actually want on a Sunday. The honest default respects the ban. The switch is yours to flip.

## Limits

One city. The time is a fixed-speed estimate, not a real cycling model, so it ignores hills, lights, and surface. Greenness is still the proxy from the last post. And the cost model has a personality: turn the car-free bonus up and it will tempt the ring road again. The current weights hold it back. They are not a law.

## The whole arc

Three posts. Five open datasets became a road network. A buffer and a formula turned that into a score per street. A discount turned the score into a route you would actually ride.

The map at the top is the payoff. Everything under it is why the green line goes where it does.

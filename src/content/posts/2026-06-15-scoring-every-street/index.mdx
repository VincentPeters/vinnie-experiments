The last post gathered five datasets into one PostGIS database. None of it is a score yet. There is a vegetation raster, some park polygons, some water, and a road network sitting next to them.

This post does the arithmetic. Every one of 40,032 Antwerp streets comes out with a single label: park, high, medium, or low.

## The question

For each road, how green is the strip you ride through? Not the road surface. The 25 metres on either side: the trees, the water, the park you pass.

The answer has to be one number per road, comparable across the whole city, cheap to compute 40,000 times.

## A 25-metre buffer

Start with the road. Buffer it by 25 metres. Reproject to the Belgian Lambert grid first, EPSG:31370, so the buffer is 25 real metres and not 25 degrees.

Then measure what falls inside.

## Vegetation, weighted

Inside the buffer, count the Groenkaart pixels by class. Tall green (trees above three metres) counts fully, at weight 1.0. Low green (shrubs and grass) counts at 0.2. Farmland and non-green count for nothing.

A street lined with old planes scores high. A street next to a mown verge barely moves.

```sql title="Vegetation fraction per road"
SELECT way_id,
       SUM(pixel_count * weight) / buffer_area_px AS veg_frac
FROM road_buffers
JOIN groenkaart_pixels USING (way_id)
JOIN class_weight USING (class)   -- 1 -> 1.0, 2 -> 0.2
GROUP BY way_id;
```

The result is `veg_frac`, the share of the buffer covered by vegetation, weighted so a canopy outweighs a lawn.

## Water and parks

Two more fractions, both from OpenStreetMap. `water_frac` is the share of the buffer inside a water polygon. `park_frac` is the share inside a mapped park.

Three numbers per road now: vegetation, water, park. They need to become one.

## One number: the combine formula

The obvious move is to average the three. The obvious move is wrong.

A street lined with trees should not beat a path through a real park. So vegetation, the cheapest signal, gets capped at half. Parks and water count in full.

The formula is a probabilistic union. Read it as: the chance the strip feels green if each signal is independent.

```python title="combine_green"
green = 1 - (1 - park) * (1 - water) * (1 - 0.5 * veg)
```

Drag the three sliders. A fully tree-lined street tops out near 0.5. Add a park and it climbs past it.

Set vegetation to 100 and the rest to 0. Green stops at 0.5, and the class is medium. A leafy street, on its own, is never high. That cap is the whole opinion of the score.

## Thresholds turn the number into a class

The score is continuous. The router wants buckets. Four of them.

A road is `park` if its buffer is at least 60 percent inside a mapped park. Otherwise `high` above 0.50, `medium` above 0.17, and `low` below. The cutoffs are not principled. They are tuned to the real distribution, where the median road sits around 0.17 and the 80th percentile around 0.51, to land a roughly 50 / 30 / 20 split of low to medium to higher.

Drag the cutoffs and watch 500 real Antwerp streets move between buckets.

## Limits

The score is a proxy. It reads vegetation off a raster and parks off OpenStreetMap. It does not know a tree is dead, or a park is fenced, or a canal stinks. The 25-metre buffer and the 0.2 and 0.5 weights are choices, not laws. The thresholds are tuned to one city's distribution and would need retuning anywhere else.
